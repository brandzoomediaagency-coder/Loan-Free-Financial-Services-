Loan settlement

A settlement (often called a one-time settlement, or OTS) is an agreement in which a lender accepts less than the full amount due, usually as a lump sum, and closes the account. It is normally considered for unsecured loans and credit cards when a borrower faces genuine hardship.

Yes. The RBI's Framework for Compromise Settlements and Technical Write-offs (8 June 2023) requires banks and NBFCs to have board-approved policies for settlements. A settlement is a voluntary agreement, so the lender decides whether to accept.

Yes. No borrower has a right to a settlement. Lenders consider factors such as how long the account has been overdue, the amount, any security, your circumstances and their own policy. A well-documented hardship case improves the quality of the request, but cannot guarantee acceptance.

Your credit report after settlement

Yes. A settled account is usually reported as "Settled", which lenders treat as a sign that the full amount was not repaid. It is generally better than an account that remains in default, but it is not as good as "Closed". See credit report guidance.

"Closed" means the loan was repaid in full. "Settled" means the lender accepted less than the full amount and closed the account. "Written-off" means the lender has removed the dues from its books, often while still trying to recover them — it does not mean the debt was forgiven.

Possibly, but it takes time. Under the RBI's compromise-settlement framework, the lender that settled with you must wait at least 12 months (for non-farm loans) before lending to you again; its own policy may set a longer period. Other lenders will see the "Settled" status on your report.

Usually only if you pay the lender the amount it waived and it agrees to update the status. Ask for this in writing. If the lender reports incorrect information, you can raise a dispute with the credit bureau. Read how to deal with a "Settled" status.

Recovery agents and harassment

Between 8:00 a.m. and 7:00 p.m., unless you have asked for contact at another time. Excessive calls, or calls outside these hours, are treated as harsh recovery practices.

RBI rules say agents should discuss your loan only with you or a guarantor, and they prohibit intimidating or harassing your relatives, referees, friends or co-workers. Record any such contact and complain in writing to the lender's grievance officer.

New RBI directions for banks and NBFCs take effect: lenders must give you the recovery agency's details at least one day before its first visit, agents must carry ID, an authorisation letter and a copy of that notice, recovery calls must be logged and recorded, and lenders must have a policy on compensating borrowers for losses from non-compliant recovery actions. Read what changes.

Write to the lender's grievance redressal officer first. If you get no reply within 30 days, or are not satisfied, you can complain to the RBI Ombudsman under the Reserve Bank – Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2026 at cms.rbi.org.in within 90 days. For threats or violence, also go to the police. See how to file an RBI complaint.

We cannot promise that calls will stop — lenders are entitled to recover dues lawfully. We help you understand the rules, record breaches, write to the lender and escalate where the rules are broken, which in many cases leads to more respectful contact.

Legal notices

It is usually wise to respond in writing within the time given, especially if the facts or amounts are wrong. Ignoring a notice can lead to proceedings in which your side is not heard. A reply should be accurate and, for court or arbitration matters, prepared with an advocate.

Under the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, the payee must send a demand notice within 30 days of learning that the cheque bounced, and the drawer then has 15 days from receiving the notice to pay. A complaint can be filed within one month after that period ends. Act quickly if you receive such a notice.

Many loan agreements say disputes will be decided by an arbitrator rather than a civil court. An arbitration notice starts that process under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. You have the right to take part and to be represented. Read our arbitration guide.
