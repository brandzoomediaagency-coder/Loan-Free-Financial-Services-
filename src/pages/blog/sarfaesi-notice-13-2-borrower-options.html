SARFAESI Notice Under Section 13(2): What It Means and Your Options

Quick answer

A notice under Section 13(2) of the SARFAESI Act, 2002 is a demand from a bank or eligible financial institution whose secured loan has been classified as a non-performing asset (NPA). It asks you to pay the stated dues within 60 days. Within that period you can pay, negotiate a restructuring or settlement, or send a written representation or objection under Section 13(3A); the lender must consider it and, if it does not accept it, communicate its reasons within 15 days. If dues remain unpaid after 60 days, the lender may take measures under Section 13(4), such as taking possession of the secured asset. A borrower can challenge those measures before the Debts Recovery Tribunal (DRT) under Section 17 within 45 days.

Few letters cause as much worry as a SARFAESI notice, because it usually concerns a home, shop or factory pledged as security. The Act allows banks and financial institutions to enforce security without first going to court — but it also sets out steps the lender must follow and gives the borrower rights at each stage. Understanding the timeline helps you respond calmly and on time, whether your aim is to repay, restructure, settle or object.

What a Section 13(2) notice is

The Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest (SARFAESI) Act, 2002 lets a "secured creditor" — a bank or eligible financial institution with a security interest such as a mortgage or hypothecation — enforce that security when the borrower's account has been classified as an NPA. The first formal step is a written demand notice under Section 13(2). It should give details of the amount payable and the secured assets the lender intends to enforce against, and ask you to discharge your liability within 60 days of the notice.

First, check the notice

  • Date of receipt — note it and keep the envelope; the 60 days run from the notice.
  • Amount claimed — compare it with your loan statement. Ask for a detailed statement of account if the figures are unclear.
  • Secured assets described — check that the property or assets listed are the ones actually pledged.
  • NPA classification — an account generally becomes an NPA when dues are overdue for more than 90 days. Read what happens after 90 days.
  • Who has sent it — the lender itself, or an asset reconstruction company (ARC) that has acquired the loan.

Your options in the 60 days

1. Pay the dues

If you can arrange funds, paying the full dues with costs and charges before the sale of the asset stops the enforcement. Keep written confirmation.

2. Negotiate restructuring or a settlement

Many lenders will discuss a revised repayment schedule or a one-time settlement, especially where the borrower shows a credible plan. Make your proposal in writing, with documents supporting your income and hardship. Any agreement should be in writing; see our settlement letter checklist.

3. Send a representation or objection under Section 13(3A)

If you dispute the amount, the classification or any other part of the notice, you can send a written representation or objection to the lender. Section 13(3A) requires the lender to consider it and, if it finds the objection not acceptable or tenable, to communicate the reasons to you within 15 days of receiving it. Send it by a traceable method and keep proof of delivery.

4. Get advice early

Because a home or business may be at stake and the deadlines are short, consider speaking to a lawyer experienced in SARFAESI and DRT matters as soon as the notice arrives.

When SARFAESI does not apply

Section 31 of the Act lists cases where SARFAESI cannot be used, including security interests in agricultural land and cases where the amount due is less than 20% of the principal and interest. For NBFCs, the Government has notified a minimum loan size for using SARFAESI (currently loans of ₹20 lakh and above, for NBFCs with assets of ₹100 crore or more). Unsecured loans such as most personal loans and credit cards are not enforced under SARFAESI.

Deadlines matter: the 60-day, 15-day and 45-day periods are short. This guide is general information about the law, not legal advice; for a specific notice, consult a qualified lawyer.
