How to Ask Your Bank to Reduce or Restructure Your EMIs

Quick answer

If your income has fallen but you can still pay something every month, ask your lender — in writing and before you default — for a change to the repayment terms. Common options are a longer tenure (lower EMI), a short moratorium or reduced EMIs for a few months, or a formal restructuring. For floating-rate EMI loans, RBI rules also require lenders to offer, at interest-rate reset, the choice of a higher EMI, a longer tenure, or a switch to a fixed rate. Your letter should explain what changed, show your current income and expenses, and propose an EMI you can sustain. The lender decides under its board-approved policy, and a restructured account may be reported as such to credit bureaus.

A pay cut, a medical emergency or a slow business season can make an EMI that was comfortable last year impossible today. Many borrowers wait until they have already missed payments before speaking to the lender — by which point the account may be overdue, charges are rising and options have narrowed. Asking early, in writing, with a clear and realistic proposal is the single most useful thing you can do.

When to ask

  • As soon as you see the problem coming — ideally before the first missed EMI.
  • When the hardship is real but temporary or partial — you can pay something, just not the full EMI.
  • Before the account is overdue for long. Lenders track overdue accounts in stages; an account overdue for more than 90 days is generally classified as an NPA. See what happens after 90 days.

If you cannot pay anything at all for the foreseeable future, a restructuring may not fit and a settlement may be discussed instead; read settlement vs restructuring.

What to put in your request letter

Address the letter or email to your branch or the lender's customer service, and keep a copy. Include:

  1. Your name, loan account number and contact details.
  2. What has changed — job loss, salary cut, illness, business downturn — and since when.
  3. Your current monthly income and essential expenses, and the EMIs of any other loans.
  4. A specific proposal — for example, "I can pay ₹X per month for the next six months, then return to the full EMI", or "please extend the tenure so the EMI is about ₹X".
  5. Supporting documents — termination or salary letter, medical bills, bank statements, GST returns for a business.
  6. A request for a written reply and for the revised terms in writing if approved.

Never share OTPs, PINs or passwords while making the request, and correspond only through the lender's official channels.

Trade-offs to understand

  • More total interest. A lower EMI over a longer period almost always costs more overall. Ask for the revised schedule and total payable.
  • Credit reporting. A restructured account can be flagged in your credit report and may affect new borrowing for a while — usually less severely than a default or settlement.
  • Conditions. Some restructurings come with conditions, such as maintaining salary credit with the lender or paying charges. Read them before agreeing.
  • Stick to it. Missing the revised EMIs can undo the benefit quickly. Choose an EMI you can genuinely pay.

If the lender says no

  • Ask for the reason in writing and whether a different proposal could be considered.
  • Escalate to the lender's grievance redressal officer if you believe your request was not properly considered or you received no reply.
  • If there is no satisfactory reply within 30 days, you may complain to the RBI Ombudsman about deficiency in service (the decision to restructure itself remains the lender's).
  • Keep paying what you can and keep records — part-payments with a written explanation are better than silence.
