Lok Adalat Loan Settlement in India: How It Works and How to Prepare

Quick answer

A Lok Adalat ("people's court") is a forum under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 where disputes — including bank loan and credit card dues — are settled by mutual agreement. Banks often send borrowers a notice to attend a pre-litigation sitting or a National Lok Adalat. There is no court fee for pre-litigation matters. If you and the lender agree, the Lok Adalat records an award, which is treated as a decree of a civil court, is final and binding, and cannot be appealed. If you do not agree, no award is passed and the lender may pursue other remedies. Carry your loan papers, statements, proof of income and hardship, and a realistic repayment proposal.

For many borrowers, a Lok Adalat is the first time a bank sits across the table and discusses a reduced or rescheduled repayment in a structured setting. It can be a practical way to close an old loan or card dispute — but because the settlement you sign is final, it pays to arrive prepared, know your numbers, and never agree to something you cannot pay.

What a Lok Adalat is

Lok Adalats are organised by the legal services authorities at national, state, district and taluk level under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987. They hear two kinds of matters:

  • Pre-litigation matters — disputes not yet filed in court, such as a bank's claim for unpaid loan or card dues.
  • Pending cases — cases already in court that are referred to the Lok Adalat for settlement.

National Lok Adalats are held on fixed dates several times a year across the country, organised by the National Legal Services Authority (NALSA). Bank recovery matters are among the most common cases listed.

Received a Lok Adalat notice from your bank?

A Lok Adalat notice is an invitation to settle, not a judgment against you. It usually names the venue, date and the amount the bank claims. It is generally worth attending: it is a chance to negotiate in a neutral setting, and ignoring it means losing that opportunity. Check that the notice names your correct account and the legal services authority holding the sitting. If you are unsure whether it is genuine, contact the district legal services authority named on it.

How to prepare

  1. Get your statement of account from the lender and check the principal, interest and charges claimed.
  2. Work out what you can actually pay — a lump sum, or instalments over a few months — after essential household expenses.
  3. Collect proof of hardship — job loss letter, medical bills, business losses, salary slips or bank statements.
  4. Carry identity proof and loan documents, and any previous correspondence or settlement offers.
  5. Write down your proposal in one line: the amount, and the dates you can pay.

On the day

  • Reach early with your documents; bank representatives usually have their own counter or bench.
  • Explain your situation briefly and present your proposal. Listen to the bank's counter-offer.
  • Do not agree to an amount or schedule you cannot meet — a missed instalment after an award can lead to the award being enforced like a court decree.
  • If you reach agreement, read the terms carefully before signing: amount, due dates, mode of payment, and that the account will be closed on payment.
  • Collect a copy of the award.

The award: final and binding

Under Section 21 of the Act, every award of a Lok Adalat is deemed to be a decree of a civil court, is final and binding on all parties, and no appeal lies against it. That is why preparation matters. After you pay as agreed, ask the bank for a no-dues or closure letter and check your credit report.
