Loan Settlement Letter: What It Must Say Before You Pay

Quick answer

A loan settlement letter (also called a one-time settlement or OTS sanction letter) should be issued by the lender itself, on its letterhead or from its official email domain, and should clearly state your loan or card account number, the exact settlement amount, the payment dates and mode, that the payment is in full and final settlement of the account, and what happens after payment — closure of the account, release of any documents or security, and the status the lender will report to credit bureaus. Pay only into the lender's official account and keep every receipt. After the last payment, ask for a no-dues certificate or closure letter.

After weeks of calls and negotiation, a settlement offer can feel like the finish line. It is not — not until you hold a written letter that says exactly what you are paying, by when, and what the lender promises in return. Many disputes after a settlement come from verbal promises, letters with missing terms, or payments made to the wrong account. This guide gives you a practical checklist to read the letter properly, the red flags to watch for, and the documents to collect once you have paid.

Why the settlement letter matters

A settlement is an agreement between you and the lender to close the account for less than the full amount claimed. Like any agreement, its terms are what is written down. If the letter is vague, the lender — or a collection agency that later buys or services the account — may claim that a balance is still due. A clear letter protects you in three ways:

  • It fixes the amount. You cannot later be asked for the "remaining" interest, penalty or charges that the settlement waived.
  • It fixes the timeline. You know exactly when each instalment is due and what happens if a date is missed.
  • It is your evidence. If the account is not closed, documents are not returned or the credit report is wrong, the letter is what you show the lender's grievance officer, the RBI Ombudsman or a credit bureau.

Checklist: what the letter should state

Read the letter line by line against this list. If anything is missing, ask the lender for a corrected letter before paying.

ItemWhat to look for
Issued by the lenderLender's letterhead or official email domain, branch or department details, a reference number, date and an authorised signatory.
Your detailsYour name and the correct loan or card account number(s). If you have more than one account with the lender, each account settled should be named.
Settlement amountThe exact amount in figures and words. If it is paid in parts, each instalment amount.
Payment scheduleThe due date of each payment and the last date by which the full amount must be paid.
Payment mode and accountHow to pay — the lender's own bank account, payment link or branch — never a personal account or UPI ID.
"Full and final settlement"A statement that on receipt of the amount the account is settled in full and final settlement, and no further amount will be claimed.
Consequence of a missed paymentWhat happens if a date is missed (for example, whether the offer lapses and whether amounts already paid are adjusted).
Credit bureau reportingHow the account will be reported once paid — usually "Settled". Know this in advance (see below).
Documents and securityFor secured loans, the release of original property documents, hypothecation or charge, and the timeline for doing so.
Legal proceedingsIf a case, arbitration or notice is pending, what the lender will do about it after settlement (for example, withdraw it or file a compromise).

Red flags in a settlement letter

  • Only a WhatsApp message or a call. A verbal or chat-only offer from a recovery agent is not a settlement letter. Ask for it in writing from the lender.
  • Payment to an individual. Any request to pay into a personal bank account or personal UPI ID is a serious warning sign.
  • No account number or a wrong one. A letter that does not identify the account cannot be relied on to close it.
  • No "full and final" wording. Without it, the lender may treat your payment as a part-payment against the full outstanding.
  • Pressure to pay today. Genuine lenders give you time to read the terms. Extreme urgency is a tactic used by fraudsters.
  • Letter from an unknown "settlement company". Only the lender can agree to close your account. Read our guide on spotting loan settlement scams.

How to pay safely

  1. Verify the letter with the lender through a channel you find yourself — the official customer care number or branch — not a number given in the message.
  2. Pay only to the lender's official account or official payment link, and use a traceable method (bank transfer, cheque or the lender's payment gateway). Avoid cash.
  3. Pay on or before each due date. A missed instalment can cancel the offer.
  4. Keep every receipt — bank statements, transaction references and the lender's acknowledgement — together with the letter.
  5. Never share OTPs, PINs or passwords. No genuine lender needs them to receive a payment.

After the last payment: NOC, documents and credit report

No-dues certificate or closure letter

Ask the lender in writing for a no-dues certificate (NOC) or account closure letter confirming that the account is closed and nothing remains payable. Keep it permanently — it is your answer if a collection call ever comes again.

Return of property documents (secured loans)

For loans secured by property, the RBI's directions on circular on responsible lending conduct (release of movable and immovable property documents, RBI/2023-24/60) require regulated lenders to release all original documents and remove any registered charge within 30 days of full repayment or settlement of the loan account. If the lender delays for reasons attributable to it, it must compensate the borrower at ₹5,000 for each day of delay.

Credit report status

A settled account is usually reported to credit bureaus as "Settled", not "Closed", because the lender received less than the full amount. That status can affect future borrowing. Check your credit report a month or two after payment to confirm the account no longer shows an outstanding balance. If the report is wrong, raise a dispute with the credit bureau; read how settled status works and how to correct errors.

Remember: the lender decides whether to accept a settlement and on what terms. This guide explains what to check; it is general information, not legal advice for your situation.
