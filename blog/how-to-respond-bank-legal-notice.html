How to Respond to a Bank or NBFC Legal Notice for Loan Default in India

Last updated: 2 June 2026  |  Loan Free Editorial Team  |  6 min read

Quick answer

Do not ignore a bank or NBFC legal notice. Read it carefully to identify the claim amount, the deadline and the basis of the demand, then gather your loan documents and account statements. Reply within the time given with a measured, factual letter that corrects any wrong figures and avoids admissions you do not intend to make. A notice comes before a case is filed, so it is also your opening to explain genuine hardship and propose a repayment plan or settlement — on terms the lender decides.

A legal notice from a bank or NBFC can feel alarming, but it is usually a letter sent before litigation — not a court order. It is the lender putting its claim in writing and giving you a window to respond. Handled calmly, that window is one of your best opportunities to correct mistakes, put your situation on record and open the door to a negotiated resolution. This guide explains, in plain Indian-context terms, how to read the notice, what to do, and how to reply.

Why you must not ignore the notice

The single most common mistake is silence. Ignoring a notice does not make the claim disappear; it only removes your chance to shape what happens next. If the figures are wrong, an unanswered notice leaves them uncorrected. If you have genuine hardship, it stays off the record. And a complete lack of response can later be pointed to as your failure to engage. Treating the notice seriously — and responding within the period it states — keeps the matter open to discussion and signals that you intend to deal with it responsibly.

Equally, do not panic. A notice is not a judgment. You have not lost any right merely by receiving it, and you are not obliged to agree to everything it says. The right posture is calm, prompt and factual.

How to read the notice

Before doing anything else, read the notice slowly, twice. You are looking for three things in particular:

  • The claim amount. What exactly is being demanded — principal, accrued interest, penalties, charges? Note how the total is broken down so you can check each part against your own records.
  • The deadline. Almost every notice gives a period within which you are asked to pay or reply. Diarise this date immediately and plan to respond well inside it.
  • The basis of the demand. Which loan or account is referred to, what agreement or document is cited, and what the sender says you have failed to do. If the notice is about a dishonoured cheque, it may invoke the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881; if it concerns a general loan default, it usually relies on the loan agreement and the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

Also note who sent it — the bank or NBFC directly, or an advocate on its behalf — and the address for a reply. Keep the envelope and any proof of the date you received it.

Gather your documents and statements

A good reply is built on facts, and facts come from your paperwork. Before you respond, pull together:

  • The loan agreement or sanction letter and the repayment schedule.
  • Your account statements and payment receipts, so you can reconcile what you have actually paid against what is claimed.
  • Any earlier correspondence with the lender — emails, letters, restructuring or moratorium requests.
  • Evidence of genuine hardship, if relevant, such as records of job loss, a medical event or a drop in income.

Reconciling the claimed amount against your statements often reveals discrepancies — interest computed differently, charges you dispute, or payments not credited. These are exactly the points a careful reply can raise.

Draft a measured, factual reply

Your reply should be calm, respectful and specific. Acknowledge that the notice has been received, refer to it by its date and reference, and respond point by point to what it actually says. Where the figures match your records, you can say so; where they do not, set out the discrepancy clearly with reference to your statements. If hardship is part of your story, state it factually rather than emotionally.

Be deliberate about what you concede. Statements made in a reply can be relied upon later, so avoid blanket admissions you do not intend to make — for example, agreeing to a total you have not verified. It is entirely reasonable to say that you are reviewing the claim and that certain amounts are disputed. Keep a dated copy of your reply and send it through a mode that gives you proof of dispatch and delivery.

If the notice is legally worded, the amount is large, or it cites a specific statute, it is sensible to have a professional read it and help frame the response before you sign anything. A considered reply drafted with proper guidance, such as our legal notice reply support, protects your position while keeping the conversation open.

Demand notice vs court summons

It helps to know, in general terms, what stage you are at. A demand or legal notice is a communication sent before any case is filed. It typically asks you to pay or respond within a stated period and is, in effect, an invitation to resolve the matter without going to court. You usually reply to the sender or their advocate.

A court summons, by contrast, is issued by a court after a case has already been filed against you. It requires you to appear before, or respond to, the court within the manner and time the court specifies. Where a notice leaves room to negotiate, a summons means proceedings have formally begun and the matter is now before a judge. The distinction is general and the exact procedure varies with the type of case, so if you receive anything from a court rather than a lender, you should seek prompt professional advice. Limitation periods under the Limitation Act, 1963 can also affect when a claim may be pursued, which is another reason to take qualified advice on your specific facts.

Using the notice to open settlement talks

Because a notice arrives before litigation, it is often the right moment to propose a resolution. Many lenders would rather agree a workable repayment arrangement or a one-time settlement than spend time and money pursuing a contested matter. Your reply can therefore do two things at once: respond to the claim and set out, factually, what you can realistically offer given your circumstances.

Be clear-eyed about how this works. You can request a restructuring or settlement, but whether the lender agrees, and on what terms, is entirely its decision. Any arrangement should be reduced to writing, and a settlement should always be followed by a written settlement letter and a No Objection Certificate once paid.
